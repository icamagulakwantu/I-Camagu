# Ulwaluko and iziduko: research review (18 Nov 2026)

What academic, institutional and community sources say, compared with what
i-Camagu has, and what could improve. Ulwaluko is sacred: nothing below
proposes publishing what happens on the mountain. Every suggestion is for the
founder and elders to accept or reject.

## Ulwaluko

### Where the app already agrees with the sources
- The sequence: preparation, the circumcision and seclusion, the first week
  with uMojiso (a goat or sheep slaughtered after the first week), coming out,
  and umgidi at home.
- Roles: ingcibi (traditional surgeon) and ikhankatha (caretaker).
- Umgidi: the elders' advice (ukuyala) and the new clothes for the new man.

### Gaps worth considering
1. **Legal steps before the season (institutional, not sacred).** The national
   Customary Initiation Act 2 of 2021 needs the initiation school registered at
   least three months before the season, and written consent from a parent or
   guardian for minors (no initiation under 16). The Eastern Cape Act 5 of 2016
   registers the ingcibi (traditional surgeon) and ikhankatha (traditional nurse)
   and sets their requirements; the provincial and national age rules differ, so
   families should confirm which applies. Suggested checklist lines:
   "Registered with the initiation committee / traditional leader",
   "Parent or guardian consent signed", "Ingcibi and ikhankatha are registered".
   These fit the app's verified-ingcibi rule.
2. **Stage names.** Scholars use four isiXhosa names: umgeno (entering),
   ubukhwetha (being an initiate), umphumo (coming out) and ubukrwala (the new
   man). The app groups the plan as "Before the mountain / Mountain phase /
   After the mountain". The founder may prefer the isiXhosa names.
3. **Ubukrwala after umgidi.** The new man's period (formal clothes, often brown,
   beige or khaki: hat, jacket, shirt, trousers, shoes) is not a stage in the
   plan; the umgidi plan only mentions new clothes.
4. **Coming out.** Sources name ukutshiswa kwebhoma (burning the lodge and old
   clothes, on or just after umgidi day). The app's "ibhoma closure" step only
   lists the courtesy for the closure team. Name only; no detail needed.
5. **Dates.** The whole period runs about three to five weeks and families fit
   it to school holidays (winter and summer seasons). A start date plus an
   expected umgidi date would plan better than a single date.
6. **Mothers.** A Rhodes study of Xhosa mothers' experiences, and the
   app's own aim, point to a short guide for mothers: what is theirs to prepare
   (umgidi, clothes, food, guests) and what belongs to the men, without
   revealing anything sacred.
7. **Other rites already drafted.** Imbeleko (a newborn introduced to the
   ancestors, with a goat) and Intonjane (a girl's rite of passage into
   womanhood, three to six weeks) are in the app as drafts awaiting review.

### Not added, by the founder's decision
- Health warnings for the first week: the founder notes the problem is not in
  established ulwaluko families in Gqeberha.
- Detail about uMojiso: named only.

## Iziduko and izinqulo

No complete, authoritative list exists online. The best published source is
Makuliwe, *Iziduko zabantu abathetha IsiXhosa* (cited by Wikipedia), which is
not online. Community lists were collected, cleaned by hand and loaded:

- 166 clans with 1,700 praise names, in `server/data/clanPraises.json`.
- Sources: abongisocial.wordpress.com (2010, with 577 comments of
  corrections), two lists on izithakazelo.blog, iziduko.wordpress.com and
  knowmyroots.blogspot.com. They copy each other in places, so agreement
  between them is not proof. Each praise records how many lists name it.
- Removed: page furniture, contributors' names, history notes, genealogy
  sentences, a garbled block, and names of kings that some lists give in
  notes (e.g. Sarhili, Dalindyebo), which are rulers, not praises.
- Praises make a clan findable in search; they never confirm someone's clan
  identity on their own. The app labels praises as community sourced.

Next steps: a review by elders or isiXhosa scholars, Makuliwe's book, and an
in-app way for members to correct a praise.
